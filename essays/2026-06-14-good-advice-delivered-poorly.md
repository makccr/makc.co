I recently finished watching [the conclusion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8KQFgWdiudo) of Linus Tech Tips's second Linux challenge. The series ended up being very popular (the conclusion racked up 2 Million view in less than 4 days), so I likely don't need to explain the concept of the series to anyone. I, was surprised however to find two of the three long-time Windows users were committed to swapping to Linux permanently in some form, and even Linus himself used Pop OS! far beyond the challenge's 30 day requirement. What I was less than surprised to discover however, is that more than a bit of the video's content was hyper-focused on criticizing the *overly-aggressive*, Linux community online. All three of the challenge's participants for example openly admitted to relying in LLM's for some amount of Linux trouble-shooting, specifically to avoid having to sift through forums or Reedit posts where they might run into the stereotypical linux-brained neck beard who silently shouts things like: "RTFM" or "You wouldn't have this problem with Arch!" Too be clear, I don't mean to make the case that the online Linux community in places like Reedit and StackOverflow is not hyper-aggressive, especially when compared to MacOS or even Windows forums of a similar nature, this seems to clearly be the case. However I do think that this particular issue is a really great way to talk about what I think is the best skill I've learned in my life: my ability to take good advice, even when it's delivered in the worst possible way. 

You see, even when it comes to the Linux community online, I have my own interesting experiences. In May of 2020 I decided to catalog my own attempt to switch to Linux full-time. I had spent the last few months falling in live with CLI apps like Vim, and for the first time thought that it might be advantageous for me to abandon the OSX set-up I had come to love. Not thinking things up super clearly I made the incredibly ill-advised decision to jump straight from a proprietary child-proof OS, straight to good ol' Arch Linux, in a two-part video appropriately titled: ["A Clueless Arch Linux Install Guide"](https://youtu.be/mxTDsqcaj1o). I say this was an ill-advised decision however, only because you wouldn't have been able to find a single person online in 2020 (and only PewDiePie in 2026) that would actually recommend a user jump straight from Windows or MacOS to an OS like Arch that requires manual installation and configuration of most of the Linux operating system. I wont' necessarily criticize the online Linux community for being rude in this essay, but I will criticize that same community for gate-keeping; the stereotypical Gentoo or Arch Linux user who will actively brag about how exquisite their set-up is, while gently reminding anyone listening that they're far too stupid to read the Arch Wiki, and should stick to safer distros like Linux Mint or Manjaro-- that's nonsense.

The truth is, jumping straight to Arch Linux was one of the best decisions I made in my life, and specifically because I did it in such a public way. Over the next year and change I fairly consistently uploaded all manner of these *clueless guides* to my YouTube channel. I focused on everything form setting up Steam's Proton & Proton GE in the early days of that software project, to hacking on various X11 window mangers, creating miscellaneous bash scripts and hyper-niche ricing projects on my new Arch Linux install. I learned an absolutely insane amount about Linux, software and computing in general in part because I thrust myself into the deep-end with tools that I barely knew how to use, but also specifically because of the Reddit, forum and YouTube comment dweller's that everyone else online seems so eager to punch down on. All the way back in 2020 (it feel's as if I might as well be talking about 1920), the option wasn't available to simply use an AI agent or LLM if I found the Reddit threads or YouTube comments to be rude. If I wanted to trouble-shoot a problem, or learn how to do something new on my system I had to dive head first into the very same waters that everyone complained so much about having to occasionally dip their toes into. I specifically even remember doing on one of the first episodes of the now defunct "My Linux Journey Podcast", in which the channel manger not only put no less than ten mid-roll adds in a one hour podcast, but also spent a not insignificant part of that one hour run-time baiting me into trashing the Arch Linux online community for being too rude to new Linux users - something that I outright refused to do.

Part of my position here could have absolutely nothing to do with the "Linux community" itself. I have worked more than a few construction, law enforcement private security jobs in my life, not to mention my younger days working in restaurants and retail. Across all of these industries, it's very rare to have anyone sit a new employee down and give them very gentle advice for how to do their job well. In retail or the restaurant industry employees learn what not to do by getting yelled at by some disgusting Karen who has nothing better to do, in construction it's a foreman or GC doing basically the same thing, during my brief tenure as a Prison Guard, I was equally likely to be cursed or literally assaulted by the inmates I was in charge of and the officers I was working with. But in all of these cases, I realized pretty quickly that generally speaking it wasn't as if anyone ever got upset for no reason whatsoever. Not every anger outburst that I've encountered in my life has been warranted, if you ask me, virtually none of them are. But that does nothing to change the fact that every outburst was prompted by something. If that outburst was directed specifically at me, I've found that it is almost always because I did something that prompted the outburst.

Throughout my years of trouble-shooting tech online, whether it be specific to the Linux community, or any number of other tight online sub-culture, I have always found that the most passionate, aggressive and down-right rude comments are often the ones that I could potentially learn the most from. One of the most obnoxious comments that people tend to complain about follows a very specific format, (a) person asks for help with X piece of software, (b) commenter berates them for using that software in the first place and tells them to use Y. This is perhaps the most common example of the type of online discourse that drives users away from sub-cultures like Linux computing, and is objectively a horrible way to go about pitching that your fellow community members use a tool that you like. But during the height of my Linux YouTuber days, this was hands down, my favorite type of comment to get below a video. Even as recently as a month ago, when I uploaded a video trashing GitHub's downfall and lamenting the fact that I didn't know of any good alternatives, I was thrilled to find that I immediately had a comment below the video providing exactly what I had said I wanted, good alternatives to GitHub:

As I have learned to expect from being in situations like this many times before, both [SourceHut](https://sourcehut.org/) and [Codeberg](https://codeberg.org/) were much closer to the kind of alternatives to GitHub I lamented not existing. Also, it's worth mentioning that even the most objectively rude parts of the comment above also just happened to be 100% true. My good microphone's audio was corrupted while recording, and rather than re-recording the video I cleaned up my laptop's on-board audio as best I could and used that - and I also 100% did leave in much more explanation than I normally would have in that particular video, as I was attempting to aim for a target audience that was slightly less tech-savvy than the audience that my videos usually reach. However, I can't ignore the fact that this is precisely the type of comment that would upset, and send many others running away form Linux, and perhaps even open-source software in general.

But what then is the answer that I'm getting at here? It's not as I mean to suggest that everyone should just accept being bullied and torn-down all the time by everyone they encounter - quite the opposite. I'm also not suggesting, despite the tone of this essay perhaps suggesting it, that there is no possible improvements to be made to Linux discourse online, that would be ridiculous. Rather, that I'm suggesting is that the simple ability to look past the tone or emotion of a statement - to separate out the substance from an insult, walking away with the new information and leaving the gross trappings that it came with behind, is about as close to a rhetorical super-power as one could hope to achieve. Would it be great if every passionate group of community members in a niche group could be gentle kind and welcoming to every interested party? Sure. It would also be great if every teacher in the world was patient, and every boss or supervisor was an incredible leader, not just a martinet who's getting paid double what everyone else is to do half the work. Unfortunately however this just isn't the reality that we live in, and it never has been. 

> "When you wake up in the morning, tell yourself: The people I deal with today will be meddling, ungrateful, arrogant, dishonest, jealous, and surly."<br> 🙡 <br> Marcus Aurelius - "Meditations 2:1"<br>*trans: Gregory Hays*

This might be a problem as old as time itself, but the good new is that there has been a know fix for as long as the problem has existed. Accept the fact that certain people - most people - are horrifically bad at articulating themselves clearly. That does not mean that every cross comment or every rude exchange is fully valueless. If we can learn to strip out the valuable content from even the most aggressive and pointless seemingly exchanges, whether they be on a Reddit thread or in real life - we have learned to become the world's best student. We don't need to have perfectly trained and well educated instructors - we don't even need people who are willing to teach us a damned thing. Our desire to learn more, and willingness to accept one of the grosser realities of human nature has already given us the ability to learn anything.
