This essay will make the argument that there is no rational basis for desiring or attempting to facilitate a state of *unconditional love*. 

Before beginning however, a few points should be made clear. **(a)** For the purposes of this argument, *unconditional love* is defined as *love* directed from one being to another that cannot cease to exist. **(b)** *Love* is defined as platonic, romantic, sexual attraction, or some combination of all three felt by one being towards another.

## Does unconditional love exist? 
Before moving any further, a fundamental question about the nature of love needs to be addressed: Can love exist, in a permeable state? Certainly the existence of unconditional love does not require any inherent rationality behind the love. It is perfectly possible to accept that unconditional love exists, because love as a matter of it's nature is unconditional, and yet also maintain that belief that this part of human nature is fully irrational. 

An easy argument for the existence of conditional love would be found in the current US divorce rate. Though the total figure has been steadily dropping since at least the early 2000s, it was still true in 2025 that as many as [43% of first-time marriages would end in divorce](https://www.forbes.com/advisor/legal/divorce/divorce-statistics/). Marriage is no sure fire indicator of a genuine feeling of love of course; but it does at least suggest a strong indication that the parties involved in a marriage at least think that they feel love towards the other party. Not only does the dogmatic and imperishable language included in most wedding vows suggest this, but so does an [average cost](https://www.cnbc.com/select/average-cost-of-a-wedding/) of well over $10,000 in The United States. Rhetorical and monetary commitment to a professed love, may not be any evidence of large spread, genuine commitment to unconditional love. But the question at hand doesn't require a wide spread trend for evidence. If in even one case, two partners had felt genuine unbreakable love, and at some point during the marriage then found that feeling gone, it would be enough to prove that unconditional love can indeed become something else.

But can love actually cease to exist? The preceding argument might *suggest*, for example, that anyone who divorce's someone they have contractually agreed to love, must no longer love said person. But this need not be true. Clearly it is possible to love someone, without a contractual obligation to do so, and to continue to love someone even if a prior cemented contractual obligation is later broken. This is particularly true if we extend our argument beyond the domain of romantic and sexual love, and into platonic love. The mere suggestion that a parent would need a contract to love a child is laughable. However, the suggestion that every parent loves their child is an equally laughable idea. How then could we explain the phenomenon of physically or sexually abused children who claim to and give all appearance of still loving their parental abuser? It seems very clear that unconditional love can exist without any reciprocation from the target of said love, but how could this trend have started? Is it possible to express love for someone if you've never had love expressed to you? If so does that suggest that every abusive parent or spouse must actually on some level love the person whom they are abusing, or at the very least have loved them at some point in time? 

That feels, at least to me, like a troubling notion. The idea that someone could torment and abuse someone that they love or loved seems to fundamentally break that common understanding of what love is. The idea that an abused child could only love someone, because they felt genuine love from their abuser is troubling indeed, but might suggest that it is fully possible to knowingly hurt people who you do genuinely love. A somewhat nicer line of questioning: could love often be based on something other than a genuine feeling, and be predicated on a status or societal norm? Would a child who loves their abusive father, still love him if he weren't their father? This line of questioning gets to the nature of how exactly love is built. Is it clearly established based on time spent with or physical proximity to another individual. If so, are we all just living lives based on a *Stockholm syndrome* form of attraction felt towards those that we profess to care the most about? 

It seems clear to me that any effort to truly understand the nature of love will leave us with more questions than answers. Me might never know why exactly we end up loving the ones that we love, or equally important, why we seem incapable of loving some individuals who have done nothing to make themselves unworthy of our love. There seems to be something spiritual in the existence of love that feels out of our control, something almost pre-ordained by some force that we have yet to define or understand, something that might very well explain how religious dogma lends itself so easily to almost every established culture's rituals and thinking of marriage andior family structures. But alas, I will have to simply close this section of the essay with a question mark. I do not know whether or not unconditional love can exist, or whether it actually does exist. For my part, it certainly doesn't feel like any outward love I've experienced in my life was anything other than the genuine article, and it certainly doesn't seem like the love is based on anything other than a real feeling of love (even though I can't seem to define what that is). At the very leas this seems to suggest that I don't suffer from any form of psychopathy (here's hoping), but it doesn't exactly help to answer the question at hand.

## Should we wan't unconditional love? 
A far more interesting question in my mind, not to mention a potentially answerable one; is whether or not we should actually desire to be loved unconditionally. It seems perfectly clear that at least some percentage of the general populus very clearly does desire this. Lines in typical Christian wedding vowels asking marriage partners to profess to love one another in "sickness and in health", "for richer or poorer" or "for better or worse" seem to further reinforce the idea that we should desire to have unconditional love form those who love us, and provide unconditional love to those whom we profess to love. We'll tackle the latter of these two claims in more detail deeper into the essay, but the former, at least to me feels patently ridiculous.
